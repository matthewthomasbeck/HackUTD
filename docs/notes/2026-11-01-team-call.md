## Grid idea: what changed

Scope:

- All-weather home monitor, with the pitch led by freezes in Texas.
  Heat waves and mold were added as year-round uses, so the box is not useful only in a rare event.
  Cayden asked for one specific case, and the answer was to sell freezes and show the rest when judges ask.
- The team said flooding is more common but was unsure how it would apply.

Target user:

- Eric proposed focusing on elderly people instead of on events.
  The call said people aged 60 and older were the most affected group in the freeze.
- Users would pre-fill age and household info, including children, for the ranking.

Hardware:

- One hub in the living room plus a small node in each room.
  Matthew would use Raspberry Pis, since the team counted only two microcontrollers and reflashing them is painful.
  Eric questioned whether each room needs a Pi for just temperature and humidity.
  Eric also found hardware in every room tedious, while the 3D home view made the project cooler.
- Matthew owns one thermal sensor, an I2C device transcribed as "MXR nine four zero", about $70 and about 1.5 weeks to arrive last time.
  Treat it as a matrix of thermometers and take the mean value.
- A humidity sensor can share the same I2C bus.
  Who has it is garbled ("Emily", possibly MLH hardware).
  Using MLH hardware does not mean entering an MLH track.
- Power must be both wall and battery.
  Matthew tested a Pi on a power bank live, and it shut off when the wall power was removed, with two different power banks.
  The Pi needs a UPS hat, and the team was searching for one that arrives in time.
  Options named: Geekworm X1200 ($48, arrives Nov 8, too late), a Geekworm hat for the Pi Zero (Nov 5 to 9), a seller with Nov 4 to 7 shipping, one arriving by Nov 6, and some with two-day delivery.
  Matthew said he must buy one right away, and the transcript does not say whether anyone did.
  Parts need to arrive by Thursday or Friday (Nov 5 or 6).
- A display screen on the hub was agreed.
  Matthew and one other person have screens.
- Matthew will bring a 3D printer for wall mounts and cases.
  The team expects to test at the Airbnb.

Software and AI:

- Dashboard: a map for responders of which homes need a check most, from rate of temperature drop, age, and household.
  Eric prefers this over AI voice calls to emergency lines, so that lines are not flooded.
- Calls could still reach residents, through the phone or through the hardware, as in Standard Response (Twilio and Cartesia were mentioned).
- The check-in flow: the box asks "are you okay?", and no response leads to escalation.
  Eric said no response alone is not enough to assume critical need.
- Matthew: time series data could feed a GRU or LSTM, even without an AI agent.
- Eric's idea: each house as an agent reporting to a head agent, which infers neighborhood outages.
  Others said it needs more purpose than that.
- Possible uses for Cayden's Even Realities G2 glasses: alerts on the display, or pointing to the window or outlet to use.
  Eric said agents may fit better with this.
- An iPhone Pro LiDAR scan or Gaussian splat could give a 3D house layout showing conditions by room.
  Camille has an iPhone Pro.

Other features raised:

- Shelters on the map.
  Cayden said this seemed like a lot of work, and icy roads mean many people cannot reach one anyway.
- Telling users when power may return, based on a rolling-outage pattern one teammate lived through.
  Cayden's objection: if grid operators cannot say, the team cannot either.
  Matthew agreed, and explained that gas peaker units could not restart until temperatures changed.
- A mold warning above roughly 55% humidity (Matthew's recollection), with a prompt to crack a window.
  Eric pointed out that a window makes a house colder in a freeze.
- Supply requests through the box.
- Charging phones from the box, which depends on battery size.
- A heater was discussed and not settled, since a powered heater does nothing for someone without one.
- Reading neighbors' posts on Ring's app to spot outages (Cayden).
- ERCOT and NOAA data for prediction, plus homes near hospitals keeping power.
  Eric expects this would not be fully accurate because some grids are older.
- A UConn weather model that plans utility crews, not help for people.
  This is the gap the team could point to.

Demo:

- Wave a soldering iron or an ice pack in front of the thermal sensor, and put a wet rag on the humidity sensor.
- The rest of the data would be simulated.

Prize target:

- A teammate said they are going for first overall, not best hardware.
  Cayden thinks sponsor tracks are more realistic, and that the top of the rankings is mostly luck.
- Nvidia is the likely sponsor track, possibly using a Jetson as the hub.
- The team is not doing an MLH track.
- The call heard that HackUTD had 19 hardware projects out of 360 last year, and LA Hacks had 20 of 300.

## Eric's install-base problem

Eric compared the project with Standard Response, which won six tracks.
That project needed no app or hardware: it got phone numbers from an emergency database, called people, asked about mobility, and sent a vehicle.
The grid map works best if many homes have the box, and that cannot be assumed.

Answers raised:

- Cayden: this is a problem for every hardware project.
  Eric disagreed.
- Build it into new homes (Cayden), or into a battery-backed thermostat (unlabeled).
  Thermostats are in most homes, but the team likely cannot access them and they fail in an outage.
- A neighborhood-wide sensor (unlabeled).
- Show boxed homes with temperature, and show outages by grid area for everyone else (Cayden).
  Eric replied that this loses the household risk data that is the point of the project.
- Use public property records and people-search sites for owner, age, and phone (Cayden).
  The team tried these on the call and found them accurate and unsettling.
- Eric's other concern: scoring still works for one home on its own, so the ranking may not need scale.

## Noticed while writing this up (not from the call)

- The team concluded it has two microcontrollers, but the group chat says Cayden has 3 ESP32 boards.
  That changes the Pi-versus-ESP32 reasoning, and Camille's earlier points (battery use and SD corruption on power loss) were not revisited.
- A UPS hat must also tell the software that wall power was lost.
  Matthew said a device has to detect this, so check that the chosen hat exposes it.
- The X1200 may be a Pi 5 board, and the team has Pi 3s and 4s.
  Check fit before buying, even with a 3D printer on hand.
- The Eric-proposed elderly focus answers Cayden's "rare event" worry, because daily checks do not depend on a freeze.
- Using people-search data for a safety product is a privacy and legal question, and judges may ask.
  Check terms of use before putting it in the pitch.
- Teammates have the personal stories the pitch needs: rolling outages, a Houston house lost to burst pipes, and an apartment roof collapse.
  Confirm who each belongs to.
